Keynote
The changing landscape and challenges of shark conservation and
management, 1980s to present.
Sarah Fowler
Save our Seas Foundation
Prior to the 1980s, suggestions that the conservation and management of sharks, skates
and rays warranted attention were more likely to raise quizzical eyebrows than to generate
action in governmental and non-governmental marine policy circles. This presentation will
review how and why this picture has changed over the decades. It will cover increased
public awareness and shark research; trends in demand for elasmobranch products and
ecotourism services; the struggle to promote and adopt fisheries management measures;
the rise of the shark conservation NGOs, and the recent inclusion of commercially-important
species in the Appendices of major biodiversity environment instruments (the Convention on
International Trade in Endangered Species–CITES, and the Conservation of Migratory
Species–CMS). While there are many achievements to celebrate, the future of shark
conservation now stands upon the cusp: failure to implement Shark Plans and to deliver
sustainable trade management under CITES threatens not only the survival of many shark
populations, but possibly also the continued engagement and support of governments,
biodiversity conventions and even the public.

 Session 1: Elasmobranchs in European Seas

 The role of the elasmobranchs in the Italian implementation of the EU Marine
 Strategy Framework Directive (MSFD).
 Massimiliano Bottaro, Scacco, U., La Mesa, G. and Tunesi, L.
 Italian National Institute for Environmental Protection and Research (ISPRA)
 The European Union Marine Strategy Framework Directive (MSFD) has the objective of
 achieving or maintaining Good Environmental Status (GES) in European seas by 2020. The
 first requirement was the “initial assessment” of the status of marine waters in relation to
 eleven qualitative “descriptors of GES”. In particular, the “descriptor 1” requires that “the
 biological diversity is maintained” and it includes elasmobranchs. In order to pursue this
 goal, Italy decided to analyse all the available information on the elasmobranchs in the
 National waters and specific attention was devoted to the analysis of data of the EU
 MEDITS project (International Bottom Trawl Survey in the Mediterranean), for the period
 1994-2011. Based on this information the “initial assessment” was performed. Moreover the
 functional group of the “demersal elasmobranchs” was proposed for two indicators to
 evaluate the GES: “condition of the typical species and communities” and the "relative
 abundance and/or biomass", and the associated metrics (the Shannon-Wiener diversity
 index and the abundance and biomass of piscivorous species). The contribution of these two
 indicators to the GES is based on temporal trend analyses. For these two indicators was
 proposed an operational target directly linked to management actions related to initiatives
 devoted to inform and increase awareness of professional fishermen on the best practices of
 handling and release of by-catches. The next key steps are the start-up of these activities
 that should be conceived in line with similar initiative defined by other EU Member States.

       Who Ray?! DNA barcoding unveils skate species diversity in ‘ray’ products
       sold across Ireland and the UK.
       Andrew Mark Griffiths, Miller, D.D. and Mariani, S.
       University of Salford, UK
       Recent food mislabelling scandals have ensured that food authenticity has a very high
       profile, with molecular methods to identify species in foods gaining renewed interest.
       Applications of DNA barcoding for identifying elasmobranch species have been very
       successful during investigations of the damaging trade in shark fins, helping inform
       management and conservation decisions. But whilst considerable effort has been spent
       identifying the species sold in shark products, little attention has focused on sales of skate
       (Rajidae).

        Skates are widely consumed across the globe, but many large species are subject to
       considerable concern for their conservation, with limits placed on the landings. Yet within the
       EU, sale of skates is still made under a blanket term of ‘ray’ with the fish only valued for their
       wings. So before sale, their bodies are usually discarded and often skinned, making
       morphological identification impossible. For the first time, DNA barcoding was applied to
       samples of skate wings traded across the British Isles. The results showed that five species
       were commonly being sold (Raja brachyura, Raja montagui, Raja clavata, Leucoraja naevus
       & Raja microocellata) and indicated that endangered species are not being passed on to
       consumers. However, the investigation highlights that the sale of skate wings under
       ambiguous labels makes it extremely difficult for consumers to exercise a right to choose
       species of less conservation concern. Additionally, in the minority of cases where ray wings
       were labelled with species names a high level of mislabelling was identified.
       Ray wings, DNA barcoding, Forensically Informative Nucleotide Sequencing, species
       identification, mislabelling.

Session 1: Elasmobranchs in European Seas

DNA barcoding of the Northeast Atlantic skates (Chondrichthyes: Rajidae),
with remarks on the widely distributed Starry Ray Amblyraja radiata.

Lynghammar A, Christiansen, J.S., Griffiths, A.M., Fevolden, S.E., Hop, H. and Bakken, T.
UiT The Arctic University of Norway
Morphological identification of skates is difficult for non-specialists due to morphological
conservatism between species and high intraspecific variation due to factors such as
ontogenetic changes. Being an understudied group, changes in taxonomy are still made and
species boundaries are debated. Here, we analysed the mitochondrial ‘barcode gene’, COI,
from a total of 175 specimens distributed in 15 species and 6 genera in the North Atlantic
Ocean. A barcode gap was confirmed between all species pairs, facilitating unambiguous
genetic identification of species. This is the first study that uses COI to successfully
separate the cryptic Dipturus batis, and the first genetic evidence on the reallocation of
Dipturus linteus to Rajella lintea. This is also the first report of Raja montagui from
Norwegian waters.
A remarkably high number of haplotypes was found in the Starry Ray Amblyraja radiata. Due
to striking differences in life history parameters, particularly size at maturity, cryptic species
diversity has previously been suspected in this group. There was, however, no concordance
between haplotype and morphotype and the present data do not support any subdivision of
the Starry Ray.

Session 1: Elasmobranchs in European Seas

The skate complex of the British Isles: current status, discard survival and
management options.

Jim R. Ellis, McCully, S.R. and Silva, J.F.

Centre for Environment, Fisheries and Aquaculture Science (CEFAS), UK
The composition and abundance of the skate assemblage around the British Isles has
changed over the last century. The largest species (e.g. White Skate Rostroraja alba) are
near extirpated from former habitats, whilst the populations of smaller-bodied species (e.g.
Spotted Ray Raja montagui and Thornback Ray R. clavata) appear healthier. Current
management regulations for skates include quota for the complex as a whole, prohibited
status for some of the more vulnerable species and, in some inshore waters of England and
Wales, a minimum landing size. The efficacy of these and other potential measures is highly
dependent on whether fish survive discarding. Here we provide a synthesis of skate fisheries
around the British Isles, the discard and retention patterns of skates in various fisheries and
available information on skate survivorship. Short-term survival for longline-caught skates
was high, although overnight soak times increased jaw damage. Skates taken in inshore
gillnet fisheries also had a high survival, ranging from 98% (<24 hour soak time) to about
88% (43–48 hour soak time). In contrast, the survivorship of skates captured by beam trawl
was lower, with about half the fish surviving. Improving our knowledge of the survivorship of
skates taken in various fisheries not only helps inform on assessment and management of
the stocks, but will also provide relevant information in relation to proposed discard bans
under the reformed Common Fisheries Policy.

Session 1: Elasmobranchs in European Seas

How many gulper shark species are there? Revisiting the North Atlantic
species diversity of a troubled deepwater shark group - the genus
Centrophorus.
Ana Veríssimo, Cotton C., Buch, R., Burgess, G.H. and Guallart, J.
CIBIO – Center for Research in Biodiversity and Genetic Resources, Portugal
The gulper sharks are a group of medium-sized benthopelagic sharks usually found on
continental and insular slopes of the Atlantic, Indian and Pacific oceans. Many of the 13-15
recognized species are routinely caught in mid- and deep-water fisheries worldwide and
some have shown a considerable decline in abundance in the last few decades. Despite the
need for efficient and sustainable management of these species, clear and consistent
species discrimination and taxonomic identification of Centrophorus remains problematic.
We used molecular (COI and 16S gene sequences) and morphometric data to re-evaluate
the diversity of Centrophorus in the North Atlantic, including the Gulf of Mexico and the
Mediterranean Sea. Data were collected from fresh specimens caught by commercial and
research vessels, from preserved specimens deposited in museum and research institute
collections, and from publicly available databases (e.g. GenBank). Molecular data separated
North Atlantic Centrophorus specimens into five well-supported groups while morphometric
measurements corroborated the existence of these five groups but indicated the presence of
three additional groups for which no molecular data were available. Comparison of North
Atlantic specimens with those collected elsewhere showed that four of the five groups
identified above also occur in the Indian and/or Pacific oceans. Our results extend the
reported range of some species considerably and suggest that most Centrophorus have
wider geographic distributions than previously reported.

Endothermy in sharks: a Comparative Transcriptome Approach.
Emily Humble, Savolainen, V., Noble, L., Jones, C. & Stefanni, S.

The Shortfin Mako shark is one of the fastest living sharks and along with all other members

cool waters. Despite the energetic cost, lamnid sharks are endothermic, where some are

niche expansion.

underpinnings have received little attention. Both functional evolutionary innovation in protein
structure, or regulation of gene expression, could result in greater metabolic activities
causing endothermy. Evidence for strong adaptive evolution in conserved metabolic genes is
rare, although has been documented where selection pressure for enhanced aerobic
metabolism has been particularly strong. Due to the extreme cost of maintaining an elevated

investigate molecular evolutionary adaptation.

endothermic sharks: the Shortfin Mako and Porbeagle, and their ectothermic sister species
the Sandtiger Shark. Functional annotation and analysis of positive selection reveal that it is
likely endothermy in sharks is governed by changes in gene expression and/or membrane
modification. This work represents a substantial increase in available shark sequence data
that will prove useful in light of an up-and-coming elasmobranch reference genome for
assessing how sharks evolve and cope with ecological and environmental challenges.

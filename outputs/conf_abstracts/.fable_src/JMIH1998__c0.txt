Feeding behavior and mechanics of sharks: past, present and future. P.J. Motta. Department of Biology,
Universny of South Florida, 4202 East Fowler Ave., Tampa, Florida, 33620 U.S.A.
Knowledge of the feeding behavior of sharks is based on a rich history of descriptive studies, and understanding of
the feeding mechanism is rooted in anatomy. These works posed as many questions as they answered. Questions
about the feeding mechanism and the function of jaw protrusion remain, the degree of stereotypy of prey capture
behavior, the role of jaw suspension in feeding, and how a relatively simple morphological system evolved to the
current diversity of feeding forms. Research is now addressing these questions.' Utilizing anatomical techniques,
computer imaging, electromyography and kinematic analysis, models of the feeding apparatus are being proposed.
Prey capture behavior appears not to be a modal action pattern, many sharks exhibiting variability and modulatory
abilities. Jaw suspension is not clearly related to feeding behavior as was once thought, and our work has revealed
evolutionary shifts that influenced the diversity of feeding forms. We are now posed with additional questions of the
evolution of suction feeding and the scaling of prey capture kinematics. The feeding behavior and mechanics of
elasmobranchs continue to be an exciting and relatively unexplored area that promises to further our understanding
of these apex predators and augment our knowledge of lower vertebrate feeding mechanisms.

Bosch and M.J. Ryan, Department of Zoology, University of Texas at Austin, Austin, TX 78712 USA.
The response of male Physalaemus pustulosus to conspecific whines of different frequencies was examined. In the
first block of playback experiments (fixed response), three types of synthetic stimuli (HFR, MFR, LFR) were used that
corresponded to calls of high frequency (mean+2 SD), mid-frequency (mean), and low frequency (mean-2 SD) for

which are at the mean or higher for the population as a whole. Nevertheless, this differential response cannot be
explained only as an effect of competition between males of different sizes, given that the relationship between
frequency of the whine and male size is very weak.

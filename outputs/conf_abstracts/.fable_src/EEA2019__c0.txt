KEYNOTE SPEAKER


                  The heart of the world's longest living vertebrate:
                                 the Greenland shark




              .

                                           Holly Alice SHIELS

                     Faculty of Biology, Medicine and Health, University of Manchester, UK
                                         Holly.Shiels@manchester.ac.uk




The life span of the Greenland shark is at least 272 years and may be as long as 500 years making
this animal the longest living vertebrate on the planet. It was one of the lesser-known species of
sharks up until 2016 when its extreme longevity was revealed. The finding that they live in the deep,
dark Arctic waters for hundreds of years has captured the imagination of the world and the attention
of scientists. How does an animal born in Shakespeare’s time still patrol the deep today? This extreme
longevity is particularly interesting with respect to the heart, because heart disease is synonymous
with aging in humans. What structures do their hearts have that permit this extreme longevity? Does
the heart underlie the ability of this shark to live so long? We investigated cardiac tissue from 8 sharks
whose ages range between ~ 100±120 to 210 ±120 years old and found significant fibrosis, evidence
of cardiomyocyte proliferation and regeneration and unusual mitochondrial biology. We did not find
evidence of atherosclerosis. These findings will be discussed in relation to the idea of chronological
age versus ‘biological’ age.

INVITED SPEAKER


                 Nervous control of ventilation and heart rate
            in the brainstem of the dogfish, Scyliorhinus canicula,
                a neuranatomical and neurophysiological study




                                      Edwin (Ted) W. TAYLOR

                            School of Biosciences, University of Birmingham, UK
                                          tedwtaylor@hotmail.com



Injection of neural tracers into selected branches of cranial nerves V, VII, IX and X that innervate
respiratory muscles and the heart of the elasmobranch fish, Scyliorhinus canicula identified their
progressive rostro-caudal distribution into discrete nuclei in the brainstem. Efferent activity recorded
from nerves supplying respiratory muscles inserted on the jaws, hyomandibular and gill arches
displayed sequential bursting activity, driving gill ventilation. This activity, which may be derive
from a respiratory rhythm generator located in the reticular formation, was relayed via the trigeminal
Vth nucleus, with subsequent activity in the VIIth, IXth and Xth nuclei reflecting their location in the
brainstem. Forced ventilation is achieved by recruitment of feeding muscles, innervated by occipital
and anterior spinal nerves.
The heart and branchial (gill) circulation in elasmobranch fishes is innervated solely by the
parasympathetic vagus nerve. Recordings from cardiac branches of the vagus showed two types of
activity: units with regular, respiration-related activity and arrhythmic activity that increased during
hypoxia and in response to J-recepter stimulation. Injection of neural tracers into the cardiac nerves
revealed that cardiac, vagal, preganglionic neurons (CVPN) have their cell bodies in two locations in
the brainstem: medial (m) within the dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus (DVN) and a ventro-lateral
scattered distribution (vl) outside of the DVN. Location of these cell bodies by insertion into the
brainstem of recording micro-electrodes revealed that m cells showed respiration-related activity
while vl cells did not. Both cell groups responded to mechanical stimulation of gill arches. Although
tonic, peripheral electrical stimulation of cardiac branches of the vagus inhibited the heart, phasic
stimulation recruited the heart at rates faster or slower than its intrinsic rate. A simple model
attempting to explain the nature and significance of some of these interactions has been proposed.

                   Growth and age validation of the thornback ray
                     in the West-Central Mediterranean basin

  Andrea BELLODI1,2, Francesca CAPEZZUTO3,2, Maria C. FOLLESA1,2, Giuseppe LEMBO4,
  Porzia MAIORANO3,2, Michele PALMISANO4, Paola PESCI1,2, Maria Teresa SPEDICATO4,
                         Letizia SION3,2, Pierluigi CARBONARA4

            1
                Department of Life and Environmental Science - University of Cagliari –Cagliari, ITALY
                 2
                   CoNISMa – Consorzio Nazionale Interuniversitario per le Scienze del Mare, ITALY
                        3
                          Department of Biology – University of Bari Aldo Moro, Bari, ITALY
                                    4
                                      COISPA Tecnologia & Ricerca, Bari, ITALY
                                                  abellodi@unica.it




Despite their evolutionary success, many chondrichthyans are increasingly threatened with extinction
as a result of human activities and the conservative life history traits of this group of fishes. In
Mediterranean basin, the thornback ray (Raja clavata), represents among the Rajids the most
commercially utilized species and it is actually included in the IUCN-RedList as Threatened species.
In particular, in the West-Central Mediterranean basin, where the fishing activity is well-developed,
R. clavata represents one of the most important by-catch species mostly for the bottom trawl and long
line fishery. Despite being a very widespread species, some gaps still exist on its biology mostly on
the growth pattern. The lack of biological data constitutes, for the Mediterranean, one of the
impediments in order to assess and take appropriate management measures for this species. Indeed,
stock-assessment models, particularly the analytical ones, require growth parameters as important
input data. The aim of the present study is to fill this knowledge gap. Specimens of R. clavata were
collected during the experimental trawl surveys, commercial landings and discard monitoring in
Sardinia, Central-Southern Tyrrhenian, West Ionian and South Adriatic Sea. Thin sections of
vertebrae were performed in order to determinate the age and calculate the von Bertalanffy growth
function parameters for both sexes in each study area. Moreover, on specimens captured in Adriatic,
Sardinia and West Ionian, the marginal increment analysis (MIA) of the vertebrae was also carried
out as a semidirect ageing analysis validation. In the Adriatic, a mark-recapture experiment, marking
by both external (spaghetti tags) and chemical (oxytetracycline injection) tags, 98 specimens of R.
clavata was also realized. In total two recaptures after 11 and 14 months were recorded with an
increment in total length respectively of 11.4 cm (from 49.5 to 60.9 cm) and 10.3 cm (from 63.5 to
73.8 cm). In both specimens recaptured the oxytetracycline mark together with the subsequent
deposition of a completed annulus (one transparent and opaque ring) was observed on the sections of
vertebrae.

 Morphometric analysis of the thornback ray Raja clavata (Linnaeus,
               1758), from the eastern Ionian Sea.

                    Archontia CHATZISPYROU1,2, Aikaterini ANASTASOPOULOU1,
                                  Constantin KOUTSIKOPOULOS2
    1
        Hellenic Centre for Marine Research, Institute of Biological Resources and Inland Waters, Athens, GREECE
                             2
                               University of Patras, Department of Biology, Patras, GREECE
                                                  a.chatzispyrou@hcmr.gr




Morphometric characters have traditionally been used to describe fish population structures and
differences between populations. They have been found to be of taxonomic importance in sex, race
and species identification as well. Knowledge involving the taxonomy, systematics and life history
of rays and skates that inhabit the Mediterranean basin is scarce. Up to date the importance of batoids
in small-scale fisheries in the Greek waters is not well-known, possibly due to the taxonomic
uncertainties concerning several taxa. The aim of this work was to present for the first time the
morphometric characters of Raja clavata in the eastern Ionian Sea, in order to identify differences
between sexes and compare them to other rajids in future references. Intraspecific variation in the
external morphology of the thornback ray was investigated and described on the basis of 35
morphometric variables, from 77 females (140-780 mm in total length) and 63 males (135-690 mm
in total length). For each morphometric value the mean, standard deviation, range and coefficient
variation (CV) were estimated for each sex separately. Regression models were applied to estimate
the relationships between each morphometric character and total length. The pattern of allometry for
each parameter was established by testing the slope (b) of the obtained regression equations against
isometry applying the Student’s t-test. (b = 1 for length-length or 3 for length-weight). The length-
weight relationship was determined according to the following equation: W=a*TLb. Differences
between sexes were tested with t-test. Further investigation on different sizes of R.clavata will
contribute to the knowledge of the stock biology, physiology and ecology.

                        Re-evaluating methods to estimate growth
                          from tagging data in elasmobranchs

            Manuel DUREUIL1,2, William AEBERHARD3, Rainer FROESE4, Boris WORM1
 1
     Department of Biology, Dalhousie University, 1355 Oxford St., PO BOX 15000, Halifax, NS, B3H 4R2, CANADA
              2
                Sharks of the Atlantic Research and Conservation Centre, Halifax, NS, B3L 2Y5, CANADA
                      3
                        Department of Mathematical Sciences, Stevens Institute of Technology, USA
                           4
                             GEOMAR Helmholtz Centre for Ocean Research Kiel, GERMANY
                                                 Manuel.Dureuil@dal.ca




The body (somatic) growth of individuals is a vital process that governs many aspects of a species’
life history. Thus, knowledge about growth parameters is critical in many assessment methods. Such
growth parameters are derived by relating individual length to age, with the latter commonly
estimated from growth bands formed in calcified structures. Recent research, however, suggests that
these structures cannot be used for aging in many elasmobranchs (sharks, rays and skates). Alternative
methods from mark-recapture tagging data have been proposed in the past, but have not been
rigorously tested on elasmobranchs. Therefore, the performance of seven different methods in
estimating von Bertalanffy growth parameters for elasmobranchs is investigated here. The
performance of the methods were evaluated in two ways: 1) Estimated growth parameters from
simulated datasets that considered individual growth variability, measurement error and different
scenarios for the length at capture, times at liberty and sample size were compared to the true growth
parameters in the simulation; 2) Growth parameters were estimated from empirical mark-recapture
tagging data for 15 different elasmobranch stocks and compared to literature growth parameters
obtained from length-at-age information. Best practicies when only limited information from mark-
recapture tagging data is available will be presented with practical applications and common pitfalls
discussed.

ORAL CONTRIBUTION


     What’s my destiny? Reproductive impairment following fishing
            capture stress in an Australian Chondrichthyan,
           the southern fiddler ray (Trygonorrhina dumerilii)

                   Licia FINOTTO1, Terence I. WALKER1, Richard D. REINA1

                   School of Biological Sciences, Monash University, Clayton, AUSTRALIA
                                           licia.finotto@monash.edu




The different effects of fishing capture are not just immediate death; unwanted animals are released
back into the environment to an unknown fate, as injuries and physiological, physical and behavioural
consequences can ultimately lead to delayed mortality and/or impact their fitness. In teleosts,
extensive research has highlighted that fisheries stress can negatively affect reproduction by
impairing individuals’ ability to contribute to the increase in population abundance and neonates’
survival and performance. Despite being one of the most threatened aquatic taxa, data on the
reproductive consequences of fishing capture for Chondrichthyans are virtually non-existent,
jeopardizing the assessment of the real consequences of this practice and the development of effective
fisheries management measures. We investigated whether trawling in semi-captive conditions
impairs the reproductive success of pregnant Trygonorrhina dumerilii, a viviparous ray commonly
caught as bycatch in southern Australian recreational and commercial fisheries. To do this we
collected data on the occurrence of stillborn neonates, parturition timing, and neonates’ dimension at
birth, growth rate and performance (boldness, reflex, swimming and burying ability). Results
highlighted that neonates born from mothers subjected to trawling simulation not only have a
significantly lower body mass at birth, as recorded in a previous experiment, but also have a different
allocation of yolk reserves and a lower growth rate, respond differently in the burying test, and swim
for shorter distances when a predator attack is simulated. Smaller size and lower growth rate might
expose neonates to a higher rate of predation, and similarly, the altered burying and swimming
behaviour may be associated with a lower ability to escape from a predator. Further investigation is
needed, but these first data suggest a possible reduction in neonates’ survival rate and parents’ fitness
that can ultimately have severe repercussions on recruitment and population abundance in affected
species.

      On the origin of the great white shark Carcharodon carcharias

Patrick L. JAMBURA1, Julia TÜRTSCHER1, Faviel A. LÓPEZ-ROMERO1, Catalina PIMIENTO2,
                                  Jürgen KRIWET1
                          1
                           Department of Palaeontology, University of Vienna, AUSTRIA
                    2
                        Department of Biosciences, Swansea University, UNITED KINGDOM
                                           patrick.jambura@gmail.com




The fossil record of modern sharks mainly consists of isolated teeth due to their poorly mineralized
cartilaginous endoskeleton and a continuous tooth replacement that forms thousands of teeth during
a sharks’ lifetime. As a result, phylogenetic analyses of extinct shark taxa are mostly based on isolated
teeth and therefore often lead to ambiguous results. The great white shark Carcharodon carcharias
is known since the late Miocene, a period in which a number of big lamniform sharks occurred, i.e.,
†Otodus megalodon, †Cosmopolitodus hastalis and †Carcharodon hubbelli, which vanished after the
Plio-Pleistocene marine megafauna extinction (2.5 mya). The evolutionary history of the great white
shark remains highly debated and two hypotheses have been proposed: (1) C. carcharias is closely
related to the megatoothed sharks, including †O. megalodon; (2) C. carcharias shares a more recent
common ancestor with mako sharks and descended from the broad toothed mako shark †C. hastalis.
Although the latter hypothesis is currently favoured, the occurrence of a narrow- and a broad-toothed
form of †C. hastalis further complicates the reconstruction of the evolutionary history of the great
white shark.
Here we report of an exceptional find of two well preserved shark skeletons of a juvenile (TL ~1.7m)
and an adult (TL ~5m) †Cosmopolitodus hastalis from the late Miocene Pisco Formation of Peru,
which display broad teeth. The presence of articulated jaws allowed us to reconstruct the tooth files
of this species and thus a more accurate phylogenetic analysis of lamniform sharks based on dental
characters could be performed, including extinct mako sharks, Isurus spp. Furthermore, geometric
morphometric analyses are performed to visualize the morphospace occupation of Isurus,
†Cosmopolitodus and Carcharodon. This combined approach allowed us to reconstruct the origin of
Carcharodon carcharias and the dental transition from fossil mako sharks to the extant great white
shark.

        Gut microbial community structure of hammerhead sharks
                   from the east coast of South Africa

             Kolobe MMONWA1, Antoinette VAN WYK2, Thulane MAKHALANYANE2
             1
              Research & Monitoring, KwaZulu-Natal Sharks Board, Umhlanga Rocks, SOUTH AFRICA
         2
             Centre for Microbial Ecology and Genomics, University of Pretoria, Hatfield, SOUTH AFRICA
                                                 lucas@shark.co.za




Sharks are the marine top predators responsible for regulating population of other tertiary and
quarternary consumers (i.e. fishes, seals and dolphins) and maintaining a naturally balanced trophic
chain in the marine ecosystem. However, there is limited understanding on how these marine top
predators are ecologically and biologically adapted to consume and digest such a wide variety of prey
items, without hampering functional traits of the gastro-intestinal tract. Gastro-intestinal microbial
community of sharks plays important different biological roles including development of vertebrae
in juveniles, metabolising a wide variety of complex biomolecules, nutrient absorption and immune
system response. Hammerhead sharks are characterised by intraspecific ontogenetic and sex
partitioning of resources (i.e. habitat and diet) as reflected by satellite tagging, genetics and stable
isotope analyses. However, it’s unknown how gut microbial biota changes between juvenile and adult
sharks (ontogenetic partitioning of resources) and also between female and male individuals (sex
partitioning of resources) within species. This study analysed gut microbial community structure and
composition of two Sphyrna spp. based on 16S rRNA gene tag sequencing at both intraspecific and
interspecific levels. DNA was extracted using Zymobiomics DNA miniprep kit and sequences
generated using paired-end sequencing on the Illumina MiSeq platform following the manufacturer’s
instructions. Quantitative Insights into Microbial Ecology (QIIME) analyses revealed non-significant
changes in microbial community structure between juvenile and adult individuals and also between
sexes within each species. The core microbiota included Actinobacteria, Flavobacteria, Protobacteria
and Firmicutes whereas Proteobacteria were the most abundant phylum. This suggests gut
microbiome of hammerhead sharks may be shaped by broader biological factors such as the host
genotype and ambient environmental conditions rather than diet.

 Marine heatwaves may elicit haematological and respiratory changes
                in juvenile small-spotted catsharks

       Maria Rita PEGADO1, Catarina SANTOS1, Dayanne RAFFOUL6, Marta KONIECZNA7,
              Ana Luísa MAULVAUT1,4,5, Mário DINIZ2, Regina BISPO3, Rui ROSA1
   1
      MARE - Marine and Environmental Sciences Centre, Laboratório Marítimo da Guia, Faculdade de Ciências da
                                       Universidade de Lisboa, Cascais, PORTUGAL
2
  UCIBIO, REQUIMTE, Dep. de Química, Faculdade de Ciências e Tecnologia, Universidade Nova de Lisboa, Quinta
                                                   da Torre, PORTUGAL
       3
         Dep. de Matemática, Faculdade de Ciências e Tecnologia, Universidade Nova de Lisboa, Quinta da Torre,
                                                        PORTUGAL
   4
     Division of Aquaculture and Seafood Upgrading, Portuguese Institute for the Sea and Atmosphere, I.P. (IPMA),
                                                    Lisboa PORTUGAL
   5
     Interdisciplinary Centre of Marine and Environmental Research (CIIMAR), Universidade do Porto, Matosinhos,
                                                        PORTUGAL.
                                6
                                  Università degli Studi di Milano-Bicocca, Milano, ITALY
                       7
                         University of Gdansk, Faculty of Oceanography and Geography, POLAND
                                                     mrpegado@fc.ul.pt




Our oceans are facing tremendous human-induced changes, including warming. As the temperature
has increased, sudden warming events designated as marine heatwaves have become longer and more
frequent with significant implications over marine ecosystems. As a crucial element of the marine
environment, sharks may also be affected by these environmental changes. Exposure to ocean
warming has shown to impact shark’s predatory behavior, metabolism and overall fitness. However,
the effects that a marine heatwave may have on these animals’ physiology has not been addressed
previously. Thus, the focus of our study was to investigate the impact of a marine heatwave (Δ3 °C)
on the heamatological and respiratory systems of a juvenile temperate shark (Scyliorhinus canicula).
To address this question, sharks were exposed to two treatments: control (18 °C) and category II
marine heatwave (21 °C) for 15 days. We then analyzed the i) normal blood cell counts (e.g.
erythrocytes, leucocytes and thrombocytes), ii) type and amount of EMNAs (erythrocyte nuclear
morphological abnormalities), iii) spleen and heart to body ratios and iv) ventilation rates (before,
initially and during the heatwave). According to our study, exposure to a category II marine heatwave
drastically changed normal blood cell counts and ventilation rates. More specifically, the number of
erythrocyte cells decreased, while the number of leukocyte and thrombocyte cells increased.
Moreover, the amount of ventilation also increased. These findings may evidence that, during a
sudden warming event, shark’s ability to transport oxygen in red blood cells, linked to their need to
ventilate more, might be significantly impaired. Furthermore, an increase of leukocytes and
thrombocytes may explain the vulnerability and stress related to the increased temperature. As marine
heatwaves are expected to aggravate with anthropogenic climate change, there is an urgency that
should eager us to manage and mitigate its impacts.

Monitoring trends in shark body size and weight is fundamental for understanding their life history
traits and elucidating population dynamics. Traditionally, size and weight information is acquired
through invasive techniques, involving capture and handling. These protocols may undermine
conservation efforts by causing physical damage, behavioral alterations, premature birth and even the
death of the captured animals. This study tested the effectiveness of laser photogrammetry as a non-
invasive technique for measuring morphometrics from five shark species housed in aquariums. The

ecotourism could be used in a wide array of scientific studies, as long as rigorous statistical methods
are applied, and the results interpreted with consideration of the sampling methodology.

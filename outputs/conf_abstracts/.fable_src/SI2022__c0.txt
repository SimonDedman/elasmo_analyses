Giulia Bressan
CoNISMa; University of Padova

Shark tale: a study of tail morphology and ecology.
1.    Giulia Bressan; CoNISMa; University of Padova.
2.    Monica Barone; Food and Agriculture Organization of the United Nation, Fisheries Resources Consultant (FAO).
3.    Fabrizio Serena; Institute for Marine Biological Resources and Biotechnologies (IRBIM); National Research Council
      (CNR).
4.    Carlotta Mazzoldi; University of Padova.

The earliest Chondrichthyes appeared 400 million years ago and have maintained their overall body shape essentially
unchanged since their origins. This long evolutionary history has brought them to develop body and fin characteristics and an
efficient swimming mode that allow them to occupy different habitats successfully. According to recent hydrodynamics studies,
compared to the other fins, the tail is the one that plays a crucial role in overall locomotor functions and its shape appears to be
correlated with the habitat occupied by the shark. Previous works have investigated this correlation analysing museum
specimens or scientific illustrations, while those that have focused on live animals were able to study only few individuals of few
species. Using photos taken from freshly fished sharks here, we investigated the diversity of the form of caudal fins in 37
species belonging to 3 orders to verify the relationships between the variability of shape and occupied habitat. Geometric
morphometric analysis was used to study the shape of the tail through the positioning of 5 landmarks. Moreover, the aspect
ratios of the caudal fins were calculated. Information regarding the habitat and bathymetric range occupied by each species
was obtained from scientific database. Relations between the occupied habitat and caudal fin shape were investigated taking
into account phylogenetic relationships.
This study contributes to the understanding of the relation between morphology and ecology in the light of the evolution of
sharks.

Keywords: sharks caudal fins; morphology; ecology

Fitting a biphasic growth model to the elasmobranch Scyliorhinus canicula (L. 1758) in the Cantabrian
Sea (NE Atlantic).
1.    Cristina Rodríguez-Cabello. Spanish Institute of Oceanography (IEO).
2.    Larissa Modica. Spanish Institute of Oceanography (IEO).
3.    Fráncisco Sánchez. Spanish Institute of Oceanography (IEO).

A number of different models have been developed to describe growth (e.g., von Bertalanffy, Gompertz, Logistic), however no
single growth model provides the best fit for describing a population growth. This study presents the results obtained by fitting a
biphasic growth model to describe lifetime growth of Scyliorhinus canicula population in the Cantabrian Sea (NE Atlantic). Data
used for all these models was based on tag-recapture database updated from a tagging program conducted in the study area
since 1993. A total of 14091 lesser spotted dogfish have been tagged during this period, 7638 males and 6453 females
respectively from which 496 were recaptured. Parameters obtained from Lester growth model (LGM) provided a juvenile annual
growth rate (h= 57.4 ±1.2 mm) and reproductive investment (g~ 0.20). Besides, after conversion, the following von Bertalanffy
growth parameters (VBGC), for sexes combined, valid only for post reproductory lifetime, were: Linf=84.5 cm; k=0.083, t0=-
2.25. Growth models (VB), such as Gompertz or logistic were compared and differences were discussed taking into account
both life history traits and fishing pressure acting on the population studied. Finally mortality estimates (M) based on previous
growth models were calculated. Parameters derived from growth models are used to estimate important life history traits.

Keywords: Scyliorhinus canicula; growth models; tag-recapture; biphasic growth; Cantabrian Sea

First evidence of sound production in wild stingrays
1.    Dr Lachlan C. Fetterplace, Department of Aquatic Resources, Institute of Coastal Research, Swedish University of
      Agricultural Sciences & Fish Thinkers Research Group
2.    J. Javier Delgado Esteban, Whale Nation Studio
3.    John Gaskell, Reef Catchments & Sharks and Rays Australia
4.    Dr Barbara E. Wueringer, School of Natural Sciences, Faculty of Science and Engineering, Macquarie University & Sharks
      and Rays Australia

Sharks, rays, and skates - the elasmobranchii - are currently not known to actively produce sounds in the wild. Here, we
present multiple records of voluntary sound production in the wild, by two species of stingray, namely the mangrove whipray
Urogymnus granulatus and the cowtail stingray Pastinachus ater. Sounds recorded from both species are characterised by a
series of very short and loud clicks and appear to serve the purpose of agonistic displays. In all recorded observations, a ray
commenced producing sounds in response to an observer approaching closely, and ceased sound production when the
distance between the ray and observer increased. Our study illustrates that elasmobranchs can produce sounds, and that more
research into this ability is required.

Keywords: batoidea; sound production; communication; agonistic display; predator avoidance

Hatching in an aquarium: first insights from successful breeding of the spotted ratfish (Hydrolagus
colliei)

10. Nicolas, Hirel; Planet Ocean Montpellier, Allée Ulysse Odysseum, CS 79562, 34960 Montpellier Cedex 2, France.

Among the numerous aquatic species displayed in aquaria worldwide, chimaeroid fishes play a special role. Not only these
scarce and delicate species are very attractive to the visitor’s education but they still have unsuspected ecological adaptations
to deep-sea environments. Knowledge of these adaptations and life traits of deep-sea species are key elements to their
conservation, even for species that are not directly endangered by anthropic activities. The basic nutritional and environmental
needs of spotted ratfish (Hydrolagus colliei) are known, however, husbandry and breeding of this species still require crucial
information to be successfully achieved. Successful breeding was first reported in Japan in early 2000 with barely one year of
survival. In Europe, the EUAC Ratfish Monitoring Program reported mating behaviors between captive H. colliei specimens as
well as egg-laying for more than two decades. However, few hatchings only were reported between 2008 and 2015, with
subsequent poor survival rates. Since 2018, Planet Ocean Montpellier (POM, France) aquarists observed an increased number
of egg cases in tanks, inducing a greater number of hatchings in 2019. To date, the oldest H. colliei specimens born in captivity
at POM are more than three years old. Since 2019, egg cases and juveniles are closely monitored and embryonic development
is regularly recorded. This presentation will focus on H. colliei husbandry and breeding at POM, in an attempt to reconcile
observations from aquarists and research. It will include environmental and developmental data that have been collected for 16
years on embryos, juveniles, and mature specimens.

Keywords: chimaera; breeding; aquarium

New mechanism for the identification of nursery areas of the Critically Endangered Angelshark (Squatina
squatina) in the Canary Islands archipelago.
1.    David Jiménez Alvarado; IU-EcoAqua, University of Las Palmas de Gran Canaria & Angel Shark Project: Canary Islands
2.    Eva Meyers; Zoological Research Museum Alexander Koenig & Angel Shark Project: Canary Islands
3.    Michael Sealey; Angel Shark Project: Canary Islands
4.    Maria Belén Caro; Angel Shark Project: Canary Islands
5.    Hector Toledo; Angel Shark Project: Canary Islands
6.    Charlotte Pike; Zoological Society of London & Angel Shark Project: Canary Islands
7.    Joanna Barker; Zoological Society of London & Angel Shark Project: Canary Islands


The Angelshark (Squatina squatina) is listed as Critically Endangered by the IUCN and the Spanish Catalogue of Endangered
Species. It has suffered a decline across Europe of at least 80% during the last century, mainly attributed to overfishing and
habitat degradation. However, a uniquely large stronghold for this species remains in an island archipelago in the middle of the
East Atlantic Ocean – the Canary Islands.
In the Canary Islands this species has an idyllic place to develop its life cycle, with no trawling or directed fishing activity, a
variety of environmental conditions and abundant prey. A key part of their life cycle occurs in nursery areas, where the offspring
are born and spend their first life stages. Since 2015, the Angel Shark Project: Canary Islands (ASP:CI) has conducted different
studies focused in these areas, designed to assess nursery are criteria established by Heupel et al. 2007. ASP:CI have then
developed a point-based mechanism to compare different sites and identify those that are confirmed, potential and possible
nursery areas.
To date, ASP:CI have discovered seven confirmed nursery areas, six potential nursery areas and 42 sites where juveniles are
present but need further study. Further, this talk will describe some of the environmental and biological drivers of juvenile Angel
shark distribution across the archipelago.
ASP:CI nursery area research was funded by Shark Conservation Fund, Disney Conservation Fund, Save our Seas
Foundation, The Biodiversity Consultancy, BIAZA, German Elasmobranch Group and WWF Netherlands.


Keywords: Angelshark; Squatina squatina; nursery area; Squatinidae; Canary Islands

Rhino rays or wedgefishes (Rhynchobatus spp) are threatened by illegal trade globally and in the Southwest Indian Ocean
(SWIO) due to their high-value fins on the international shark-fin market. Rhynchobatus australiae and Rhynchobatus
djiddensis are classified as Critically Endangered on the International Union for Conservation of Nature (IUCN) Red List of
Threatened Species, yet little is known about the distribution of genetic diversity and populations across the SWIO region. This
hinders assessments of population trends that are required for effective conservation strategies. Previous molecular species

Tanzania and Seychelles were identified as R. australiae. Here we investigated the genetic diversity and connectivity within and
between species and sampling locations using sequence data from three mitochondrial gene regions (cytochrome oxidase I,
nicotinamide adenine dehydrogenase subunit 2, mitochondrial control region) and 9 nuclear microsatellite markers developed
from high-throughput sequencing data. We also developed molecular resources to aid in accurate species identification and

eDNA (environmental DNA) metabarcoding assays based on the 12S and 16S ribosomal RNA gene regions. This knowledge
could further the genetic characterization of populations of these and related species and promote the taxonomic resolution of
the Rhynchobatus species complex.

Héctor Toledo Padilla
Angel Shark Project: Canary Islands

The value of scientific data, citizen science, and collaboration with stakeholders in developing a
Recovery Plan
1.    Héctor Toledo; Angel Shark Project: Canary Islands
2.    Eva Meyers; Zoological Research Museum Alexander Koenig & Angel Shark Project: Canary Islands
3.    Joanna Barker; Zoological Society of London & Angel Shark Project: Canary Islands
4.    David Jiménez-Alvarado; Universidad de Las Palmas de Gran Canaria & Angel Shark Project: Canary Islands
5.    Charlotte Pike; Zoological Society of London & Angel Shark Project: Canary Islands
6.    Michael Sealey; Angel Shark Project: Canary Islands
7.    Belén Caro; Angel Shark Project: Canary Islands

The Canary Islands have been identified as a unique stronghold for the Angelshark, Squatina squatina, because it can be seen
relatively frequently by divers and fishers. The species is highly susceptible to accidental capture in fisheries and habitat
degradation, due to its benthic ecology and coastal location. This has caused the species to be listed as Critically Endangered
on the IUCN Red List, and action is required for its protection.
In 2019, Angelsharks were listed in the Spanish Endangered Species List for Canary Islands waters, under “In danger of
extinction” (highest category within this legislation). In accordance with this listing, it was required that a Recovery Plan be
developed, including appropriate measures for the management and conservation of Angelsharks.
Angel Shark Project: Canary Islands (ASP:CI) ran a series of in-person and online surveys to collate information on Angelshark
interactions, perceptions and possible measures to improve their protection with three main user groups: divers, recreational
fishers and commercial fishers. This information was used alongside ASP:CI scientific data and a collaborative workshop with
stakeholder representatives to identify the location of conservation areas and co-design specific conservation measures in
order to safeguard the future of Angelsharks in their uniquely large stronghold. This presentation will outline our key findings
and the importance of using a participatory approach to design conservation measures for a Critically Endangered species.
This work, led by ASP:CI, was funded by Shark Conservation Fund and through a contract with the Canary Islands
Government, co-financed by the FEDER 2014-2020 Operational Program.

Keywords: Angelshark; Canary Islands; recovery plan; co-design; conservation measures

Dichlorodiphenyldichloroethylene (DDE), a legacy agrochemical metabolite, in the Critically Endangered
Brazilian guitarfish Pseudobatos horkelii from Southeastern Brazil
1.    Renata Daldin, Leite; Universidade Federal do Paraná (UFPR)
2.    Ana Paula Chaves, Silva; Universidade de São Paulo (USP)
3.    Eloísa Pinheiro, Giareta; Universidade Federal do Paraná (UFPR)
4.    Natascha, Wosnick; Universidade Federal do Paraná (UFPR)
5.    Natalia ,Guimarães; Universidade Federal do Rio de Janeiro (UFRJ)
6.    Amanda Pontes, Lopes; Fundação Oswaldo Cruz (Fiocruz)
7.    Francielli Casanova, Monteiro; Pontifícia Universidade Católica do Rio de Janeiro (PUC-Rio)
8.    Carlos, Massone; Pontifícia Universidade Católica do Rio de Janeiro (PUC-Rio)
9.    Renato, Carreira; Pontifícia Universidade Católica do Rio de Janeiro (PUC-Rio)
10.   Marcelo, Vianna; Universidade Federal do Rio de Janeiro (UFRJ)
11.   Rachel Ann, Hauser-Davis; Fundação Oswaldo Cruz (Fiocruz)


Dichlorodiphenyltrichloroethane (DDT) is a broad-spectrum pesticide banned in Brazil since 1998. One of its main metabolites,
dichlorodiphenyldichloroethylene (DDE), is highly persistent, fat-soluble and extremely toxic to aquatic organisms. Scarce
studies in elasmobranchs are available, and none have been performed for the Brazilian guitarfish Pseudobatos horkelii.
Herein, DDE concentrations were determined in the livers of 22 Brazilian guitarfish individuals, five non-pregnant adult females,
two pregnant females, 10 embryos (eight females, two males) and five adult males and correlated to the hepatosomatic index
(HSI), gonadosomatic index (GSI) and condition factor (CF) for all groups. Maternal transfer was confirmed, as embryos
contained detectable amounts of DDE, albeit lower than in all adult groups. Higher concentrations were found in adult males
compared to non-pregnant adult females and embryos, and intermediate values were observed for adult pregnant females. The




                                                                   Sharks International 2022                         Page        7
higher values observed in males may reflect the maternal contaminant offloading processes through female ovulation and
pregnancy processes, resulting in lower DDE concentrations, as noted for other elasmobranchs. The positive correlations noted
between DDE and the IHS and negative correlations between DDE concentrations and the IGS for all groups (including
embryos) indicate potentially deleterious systemic health effects for the liver and reproductive systems, respectively, beginning
in the womb and indicating serious concerns for the species. Finally, negative correlations between DDE concentrations and
the FC indicate that this pesticide metabolite may result in potential general health effects. This is the first study to assess DDE
concentrations and maternal offloading processes in this Critically Endangered species.


Keywords: legacy agrochemicals; organochlorine pesticide; maternal contaminant offloading; DDE; morphometric indices

Ryan J. Nevatte
School of Natural Sciences, Macquarie University, North Ryde, New South Wales, Australia

Population structure of commercially fished sawsharks in southern Australia
1.    Jane E. Williamson; School of Natural Sciences, Macquarie University, North Ryde, New South Wales, Australia
2.    Barbara E. Wueringer; School of Natural Sciences, Macquarie University, North Ryde, New South Wales, Australia;
      Sharks and Rays Australia, PO Box 575, Bungalow, Queensland, Australia
3.    Michael R. Gillings; School of Natural Sciences, Macquarie University, North Ryde, New South Wales, Australia

Sawsharks are demersal sharks characterised by a distinctive saw-like rostrum with a pair of long barbels on the ventral
surface. In Australia, two sawshark species – the common (Pristiophorus cirratus) and southern (P. nudipinnis) sawshark – are
frequent by-catch in commercial fisheries and sold for human consumption. Although harvesting is currently considered
sustainable, much of the biology of these sharks is poorly understood. In particular, it is unknown whether these species display
genetically distinct populations throughout their ranges. Such information is necessary for effective management decisions in
fisheries.
We examined whether P. cirratus and P. nudipinnis display genetically distinct populations in the waters of southern Australia.
Analysis of two mitochondrial genes (Cytochrome-b and ND5) indicated that the two species are structured differently, with P.
cirratus consisting of two populations and P. nudipinnis consisting of one population. Tests for population expansion also
highlighted differences between the species. Signatures of population expansion were detected in P. nudipinnis whilst P.
cirratus showed an overall signature of stable population size, with evidence of expansion in one mitochondrial lineage. The
observed population structure and expansion signatures are likely due to oceanographic changes in southern Australia during
the Pleistocene. These results provide important information for management of sawsharks in commercial fisheries and
demonstrate that population structure in closely related species within the same region can differ, and therefore requires
investigation at the species level.

Keywords: population genetics; fisheries; Pristiophorus; biogeography

The Shark Trust

Conservation planning for angel sharks in the Mediterranean
Cat A Gordon, Ali R Hood

Angel sharks (Squatinidae) are amongst the most threatened families of chondrichthyan species, with 13 of the 23 described
species in a threat category. Reliance often falls on reports of incidental catch, citizen science, social media, and interviews
with fishers to increase knowledge of distribution. Over recent years, in the wake of the Eastern Atlantic and Mediterranean
Angel Shark Conservation Strategy and inception of the Angel Shark Conservation Network, many more records have been
unearthed from the Northeast Atlantic and Mediterranean.
The Mediterranean Angel Sharks: Regional Action Plan sets out a roadmap for the three Critically Endangered species
encountered in this region - Sawback Angelshark (Squatina aculeata), Smoothback Angelshark (S. oculata), and Angelshark
(S. squatina). SubRegional Action Plans have since been developed for the Aegean Sea, Northern Levant Sea, Cyprus,
Southern Ionian Sea and Tunisia, with a view to facilitating further coordinated action by engaging regional stakeholders (e.g.
governments, industry) to better protect angel sharks.
Varying levels of management and compliance in each region means key policy actions are adapted according to requirement.
In working with regional partners, focus is on advancing policy developments, implementing existing regulations, and improving
fisher awareness/engagement to reduce bycatch mortality. Collaborative action is essential to build capacity for angel shark
conservation.
Through the development of SubRegional Action Plans, along with the global angel shark ID guide, it’s hoped that a spotlight
will be shone on this threatened family and highlight the importance of catch monitoring and management measures to prevent
other species from following declining trends.


Keywords: angel sharks; Squatinidae; implementation; conservation planning; Mediterranean

Fisheries of the cryptic angelshark (Squatina spp.) in northern Peru
1.    Rossana Maguiño Napurí: ecOceánica
2.    Daniella Biffi: ecOceánica, Texas Christian University
3.    Keny Kanagusuku: Universidad Científica del Sur
4.    Alejandra Mendoza: ecOceánica
5.    Renán Flores García: Instituto Tecnológico Contralmirante Manuel Villar Olivera
6.    María Isabel Sanjinez Medina: Instituto Tecnológico Contralmirante Manuel Villar Olivera

8.    Dean Williams: Texas Christian University

Worldwide, angel sharks (family Squatinidae) are the third most threatened family of chondrichthyans. In the Southeast Pacific,
two species are recorded: Squatina californica and Squatina armata. In Peru, between 1996 and 2001, the sixth most fished
shark species was the S. californica. The catch of S. californica in Peru has declined by more than 90% between 2001 and
2020. A recent study conducted in Peru, confirmed the presence of S. californica and S. armata, and reported a potential third
undescribed cryptic species (Squatina sp.). This study aimed to characterize the biology and fisheries of Squatina spp. in
northern Peru. Five landing sites were evaluated between 2017 and 2019 (552 days). A total of 1,565 angelsharks were
recorded (average total length 84.8 cm [SD=13.0, range: 27.7-101 cm], 56% female). Ninety-seven percent of the landings
were from Puerto Pizarro (PP), the most northern site. Ninety-nine percent of the individuals landed in PP (n = 337) came from
fishing areas in Ecuador. Tissue samples of 110 individuals were barcoded using the mitochondrial cytochrome c oxidase I
(COI) gene. Sixteen samples were identified as S. californica and 94 as S. sp. Most of the S. sp. samples were from Puerto
Pizarro (n = 78). The results are concerning since direct capture of sharks has been prohibited in Ecuador since 2007.
Moreover, the study highlights the need for accurate landing reports in Peru given the steep decline of the total catch of
angelsharks.

Keywords: Ecuador; DNA barcoding; illegal; bycatch; pregnant females

Long-term catch trends and risk assessment of the Critically Endangered white-spotted wedgefish
(Rhynchobatus djiddensis) from South Africa.
1.    Ryan, Daly; Oceanographic Research Institute & South African Institute for Aquatic Biodiversity.
2.    Denham, Parker; Department of Environment, Forestry and Fisheries & University of Cape Town.
3.    Geremy, Cliff; KwaZulu–Natal Sharks Board & University of KwaZulu–Natal.
4.    Gareth, Jordaan; Oceanographic Research Institute.
5.    Nkabi, Nomfundo; KwaZulu–Natal Sharks Board.
6.    Rhett Bennett; South African Institute for Aquatic Biodiversity & Wildlife Conservation Society.
7.    Bruce, Mann; Oceanographic Research Institute.


The white-spotted wedgefish (Rhynchobatus djiddensis) is a Critically Endangered shark-like ray in the family Rhinidae.
Throughout its Western Indian Ocean distribution, it is targeted for its valuable meat and fins and is reported to have undergone
major population declines. This study used two independent long-term (37 and 40 years) time-series catch data from
competitive shore angling and shark nets to investigate the size composition and catch per unit effort (CPUE) and conduct a
risk assessment for the population on the east coast of South Africa. From 1977 to 2017 the competitive shore fishery captured
7,703 individual R. djiddensis, whilst shark nets in the same region captured 2,856 individuals from1981 to 2017. Although the
mean annual sizes of net-caught individuals were above the size of reported sexual maturity, there was little evidence to
suggest that any individuals captured were reproductively active. Standardized CPUE from the competitive shore fishery
declined substantially between 1977 and 2017 and shark net catches exhibited a significant (p< 0.05) fourfold decline in annual
nominal CPUE from1981 to 2017. Ultimately, a risk assessment showed a 65.1% decline in abundance over a period of three
generation lengths, which indicates that the sampled population of R. djiddensis in South Africa should be classified as
Endangered. It is critical that the drivers of this population decline are identified and addressed to improve the conservation
outcomes for this extremely vulnerable species.

Keywords: wedgefish; Critically Endangered; West Indian Ocean Conservation

1.    Sebastian, Kraft; Centre of Marine Sciences (CCMAR), University of the Algarve, Campus de Gambelas, Faro, Portugal
2.    David, Abecasis; Centre of Marine Sciences (CCMAR), University of the Algarve, Campus de Gambelas, Faro, Portugal

Marine protected areas (MPAs) are regarded as a useful tool to protect elasmobranchs. However, these species are generally
not a primary target when designing MPAs and how they benefit from these remains to be better understood. To this end,
acoustic telemetry has shown to be a very fitting tool. The Arrábida marine park is a temperate coastal MPA off the Portuguese
West coast, near two important fishing ports, Sesimbra and Setúbal. The common stingray (Dasyatis pastinaca) is a medium-
sized viviparous ray found mostly in shallow waters from Norway to Senegal and the Mediterranean. This species is caught
throughout its range and is currently classified as vulnerable by the IUCN. To study their space use and residency in this MPA,
28 individuals were tagged with acoustic transmitters and monitored for an average of 625 days and up to over 1000. Average
residency was low (Residency Index=0.211) ranging from RI=0.002 to RI=0.588. Their presence in the marine park was
markedly seasonal, highest during the coldest months and with virtually all individuals leaving during the warmer months of the
year. During their absence, individuals were detected in the nearby Sado estuary, where they likely remained for reproductive
purposes as suggested by the literature on Dasyatis. This pattern was repeated at least twice by seven individuals during the
entire monitoring period. These results show common stingrays are not protected year-round by this marine park, which they
leave during the reproductive season. We discuss additional measures that could be considered to improve their protection.

Shark bycatch and depredation occurs frequently in the offshore marine parks surrounding Lord Howe Island, Australia. This
causes loss of bait, fishing gear and catch for fishers and sharks can be injured or killed when bycaught. To understand this
shark-fisher conflict and identify ways to mitigate it, we applied a multi-disciplinary approach including acoustic telemetry,

interactions. The novel multi-disciplinary approach used in this case study should be adopted elsewhere to help characterise
